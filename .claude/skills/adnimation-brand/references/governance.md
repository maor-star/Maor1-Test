# Governance — names, approvals, partnerships, announcements

## The names rule

Overrides everything else in the skill.

Any content mentioning **any** name requires manual approval from the user before
publishing. Every single time. Previous approval of a name in one post never covers
another post about the same entity.

A name includes: company names, brand and third-party product names, domains,
personal names, event names, acronyms, logos, screenshots where a name appears, and
indirect descriptions that identify unambiguously.

An item that would otherwise publish independently becomes an approval item the
moment a name enters it. This does not block name-free professional content,
activity explanations, category-level reviews, or technology content — the bulk of
routine output still runs independently.

### Why this is stricter than it looks

Most publisher contracts prohibit name or logo use outright. A contact who says "sure,
go ahead" often has no authority to waive that, and their enthusiasm is not a
contractual permission. The verification is against the **contract**, not the person.

## The approval chain

| Party | Required | The trap |
|---|---|---|
| Publishers and clients | Their own written approval, verified against the contract | Verified against the contract, not the contact |
| Demand and technology partners | Mutual sign-off on exact wording, plus confirmation the integration is actually live | Announcing an integration that has not gone live |
| Any logo use | Separate approval | Text approval is not logo approval |
| Scope of claim | Must match the signed contract | "We work with X" and "X chose us" are legally different statements. Never imply exclusivity, scale or depth beyond what is contracted |
| Silence | Is not approval | No reply within a week means no publication |

## Partnership tiering

Partnership volume is high; selection is the job. Cap: at most **one** dedicated
announcement post per week.

**Tier 1 — its own post.** A name the market recognizes, or a connection that
materially changes what the company can offer. Maximum 2–4 per month.

**Tier 2 — monthly roundup.** "What we connected this month", 5–8 lines, one
connection per line with what it enables. This is the point of the tiering, not a
consolation prize: it turns volume itself into evidence of momentum instead of noise.

**Tier 3 — not published.** Logged internally, used as raw material for professional
content later.

**A big name does not automatically earn Tier 1.** A flagship publisher or major
demand platform is worth far more as a full success story after 60–90 days than as a
one-line launch announcement. When there is a choice, wait and tell what happened.
The launch post is the weakest thing you can do with a strong name.

## Announcement templates

These are fixed. Select one; never redesign one for a specific announcement.

### New hire — five lines

1. Name and role
2. One line on where they came from
3. One line on what they will do
4. One line on who in the audience it matters to
5. One line of welcome, or an approved quote

**Identical length and identical enthusiasm at every seniority level.** A director
and a junior analyst get the same word count and the same tone — the template does
not scale with title, and people notice immediately when it does. One photo, same
visual treatment for everyone.

Never name a competitor they left. "Ten years on the sell side" is fine; the company
name is not. Never mention compensation.

### Partnership

1. What the partnership enables, concretely
2. Who in the audience it matters to
3. Optional approved quote from the other side

Never publish before written sign-off from the partner on exact wording. Never
disclose commercial terms, volumes or exclusivity. Never imply broader scope than
signed.

### Success story — four parts

1. **The starting constraint or number** — where they were, and what was in the way
2. **Two or three concrete actions taken** — actions, not adjectives
3. **The result, with timeframe, and what held over time** — a number that decayed in
   month three is a different story than one that held
4. **One lesson useful to a non-customer** — if a reader who will never buy gets
   nothing, the story is not finished

Real numbers only. Blurring is fine — "a content network at roughly 3M monthly
impressions". Invention is not. Never present a one-off result as typical; if a
result is above our average, the post says so.

Success stories work for all three audiences with the same structure — one of the
few formats that does, which is why the slot is worth protecting even when the
material is slow to arrive.

## Visual consistency

One approved palette, one type family, fixed logo position, fixed aspect ratios
(1200×627 standard, 1080×1080 square). Seven frozen templates: quote, new hire,
partnership, success story, stat, event, article. Select a template, never redesign.
No generic stock imagery, ever.

### Dashboard screenshots

Ask the user for a real screenshot before building any post that relies on one.
Never invent or reconstruct an interface.

Before publishing, screen for client names, domains, account identifiers, absolute
revenue figures, demand partner names, or anything identifying a client. Never show
screens that reveal internal logic, signal names or data structure. Prefer a tight
crop over a full screen, and relative trends over absolute numbers. Identical visual
treatment on every screenshot.

## Events

Real photos from the field only, never stock. No photo identifying an outside person
without their consent. No screens, price sheets, client names or documents visible.
Captions say what was discussed or learned.

Cadence: before (where we will be), during (one update per day), after (2–3
takeaways). The "after" post is the most valuable and the most often skipped.

## Category analysis

Map categories of solutions, not a list of names to attack. Explain which approach
suits whom, **including cases where Adnimation is not the right fit** — that is what
makes it credible rather than a brochure, and it is the part most likely to be cut
under pressure. Keep it.

When naming a player, use only verifiable public facts, neutral tone, with a source
link. Never a negative word about a named competitor.

Do not declare category leadership. Demonstrate it through positions others cite,
data only we have, and terms the market adopts. Arguing a position is encouraged;
announcing victory is not.

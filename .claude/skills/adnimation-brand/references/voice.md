# Voice — worked examples and the harder calls

The voice is easy to state and easy to lose. What follows is mostly about the ways
it gets lost.

## The test

Would you send this sentence to a peer who runs ad ops at a mid-sized publisher, in
a direct message, expecting to be taken seriously? If it would read as a pitch there,
it reads as a pitch everywhere.

## Before and after

**Opening lines.** The first line is read alone. It has to carry a claim.

> ✗ In today's fast-paced digital landscape, publishers face many challenges.
> ✓ Cutting ad units to fix Core Web Vitals is a revenue decision, and it is usually made without revenue data.

> ✗ Let's talk about price floors. 🧵
> ✓ Most audience signal on a bid request is an inference drawn from a URL.

**Claims.** Specificity beats intensity every time.

> ✗ Our revolutionary AI unlocks game-changing revenue growth.
> ✓ Pricing that moves while the market moves.

> ✗ We leverage cutting-edge technology to supercharge yield.
> ✓ The technology does the work and our team runs it.

**Endings.** End on a statement. A generic question at the end reads as a request for
engagement rather than a finished thought.

> ✗ What do you think? Let me know in the comments!
> ✓ Both numbers already exist. They are just sitting in different systems.

## Acknowledging trade-offs without hedging

The brief asks for trade-offs to be acknowledged. The failure mode is hedging the
whole piece into mush — qualifying every sentence until nothing is claimed.

The move is to make the trade-off a **specific, bounded admission** and then continue
asserting. One clean concession buys credibility for everything around it.

> ✓ We took the other side of that. The technology does the work and our team runs
> it. That is a harder business to operate and a slower one to scale. It is also the
> only version where a publisher's revenue does not depend on their own free time.

The middle sentence gives up something real — the business is harder and scales
slower. The next sentence does not retreat.

Contrast with the mush version:

> ✗ Our approach may not be right for everyone, and there are certainly cases where
> other solutions could potentially work better, but we believe it can help some
> publishers in certain situations.

That concedes nothing specific and claims nothing either.

## Arguing a position without attacking anyone

Positions are encouraged. Named criticism is prohibited. The way through is to argue
against a **practice or a category**, never a company.

> ✓ A yield tool nobody operates is a reporting tool.
> ✓ Software has no opinion about your inventory. It executes.

Both are pointed. Neither names anyone, and neither could be read as targeting a
specific competitor.

If a draft only works because the reader will infer who is meant, it fails — an
unambiguous indirect description counts as a name.

## Numbers

Specific numbers when you have them. When you do not, do not reach for a vaguer
version of the same claim — drop the claim.

> ✗ up to 50% revenue growth
> ✗ significant revenue improvement
> ✓ [nothing — write the post without the number]

"Significant" is not a safer version of a number you cannot substantiate. It is the
same claim with the accountability removed.

Illustrative arithmetic is allowed when it is labelled as illustrative and the
numbers are obviously arbitrary — a worked example showing the *shape* of a
calculation. It must never be presented as our data or a client result.

## Failure modes that slip through most often

**The mechanism creep.** A piece feels thin, so a sentence gets added explaining how
something works. Add depth to the *problem* instead. The problem is unlimited; the
mechanism is off-limits.

**The positioning restatement.** With no market input, content drifts toward
restating the pillars in new words. It reads as a brochure within about three posts.
The tell is that the piece could have been written a year ago, or by a competitor
with the nouns swapped.

**The engagement question.** Ending on "what do you think?" after a confident piece
undoes the confidence.

**The stacked hashtag.** Three is the cap and three is not a target. Two precise ones
beat three where the last is filler.

**The enthusiasm gradient in announcements.** A senior hire gets a warmer post than a
junior one. Everyone in the company notices. Same length, same tone, every time.

## On Hebrew and English

Content publishes in English — the audience is primarily US. Internal working
documents, planning notes and conversation with the user may be in Hebrew. Never mix
the two inside a published asset.
